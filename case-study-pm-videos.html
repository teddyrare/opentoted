The Guy from the PM Software Videos

When we canceled our most-requested training subject without a backup plan, I wrote, produced, and starred in a video series to fill the gap. The project management software has been renamed, the pirate hat was real.

Chapter 1:

Overload Problem
01

At Apple, managing projects can be very robust. "Robust" is an Apple-y way of saying "Incredibly Complicated." There were several ways folks managed projects: some used an internal bug tracking app, others used Slack. The official internal project management tool was Jolly Roger. Was it actually called Jolly Roger? No. But for the sake of brevity and covering my confidentiality bases, let's pretend that it is.

Jolly Roger was robust. It was also intimidating to start using, and one of the most commonly requested private group training topics for my team. We had a series of live classes, written by a colleague for the initial rollout. That colleague (we'll call her Anne B., a different Anne than you may have met in the Learner Interviews piece) and I were the only folks who taught Jolly Roger regularly. It was a complex app that generated a lot of questions, and frankly, it intimidated our other trainers. Jolly Roger was dry, robust, and scary. People had a hard time learning it, and our trainers had a hard time teaching it.

It was no surprise nobody else wanted to teach it. I initially learned the material by taking the courses, then deepened my knowledge by teaching them and fielding questions. I spent about a year running private sessions and sharing the workload with Anne B. We taught a lot of Jolly Roger on top of everything else. We were feeling the squeeze.

Chapter 2:

"Let's Pause (Cancel) These Courses"
02

I was called into a virtual meeting with my manager and a few other team members. When the meeting kicked off, my manager announced that he wanted to cancel all of the Jolly Roger live classes. This was surprising. I knew these classes were pretty well attended. Anne B. was on a rotation at the time, so most of the courses were being assigned to me anyway. My hope going in was that we'd find a way to bring more trainers up to speed on the material, not abandon it entirely.

"Um, why are we planning to cancel these courses? It's pretty surprising to hear this decision considering I don't think anyone else in this meeting teaches Jolly Roger." This was true. I was the only person in that room who taught this course. In retrospect, I definitely could have added a bit of sugar with that medicine. "Well, this is the decision we're making," my manager said. "We want to free up bandwidth so trainers can focus on other projects."

"The trainers" was me, and I wasn't convinced. "Right, but if you cancel the classes, won't more people just request private training sessions?" "Good point, Ted. We'll just have to wait and see."

So the decision was made. We were canceling Jolly Roger classes and would see how many private session requests came in afterward.

"Ok, so since we're pausing these courses, do we have a video series or self-guided offering we can point people to?" I asked. "That's a great question," my manager said.

We didn't. I shared my opinion, we were moving forward with the plan in place, and I had no backup to offer learners.

I was concerned about my workload. I saw a lot of private Jolly Roger sessions on the horizon.

Chapter 3:

The Pivot
03

Over the next few months, my concerns from the meeting were validated. We waited, and we saw. I was teaching a lot of private Jolly Roger sessions, and I knew we needed something to act as a secondary layer of support. The questions coming in were repetitive. People were hitting the same walls. While I was learning more about Jolly Roger than I ever expected, I knew the current setup wasn't sustainable.

Anne B. came back from her rotation, but I had a feeling she wouldn't be on our team much longer, a feeling that proved correct a few months later when she accepted a role with her rotation team.

I was now the SME of Jolly Roger by default, and I knew we needed to do something bold to fill the gap. I set up a meeting with the same group as before.

I had an idea.

Chapter 4:

Writing the Series
04

The idea was to do a video series about Jolly Roger. The video team had done a few videos like this before, but this would be the largest scale we had attempted. Concerns were minimal. I opened with the proposition that I would write it and be on camera.

I also wanted to do something different than just converting our live classes into video. Instead, I proposed two levels: Introductory and Going Further. Jolly Roger had a lot of features, so the plan was to build a baseline series as a foundation, then follow it with more advanced content and follow-along demos of the nuanced stuff. The idea was approved, and I was off to write it.

The writing process was tedious. My team used Numbers for video scripts, which made everything unnecessarily difficult. You had to press Option + Return to add a new line inside a cell, and copy-pasting images into the same table was a nightmare. Imagine trying to write something while the words wrestle each other and your cursor gets drop-kicked to a random line. This was the system in place, and I had to work within it.

I also wanted learners to follow along with the demos rather than just watch. That meant testing every activity myself before scripting it: another layer of complexity to manage inside the Numbers Thunderdome already open in my other window.

The last piece was the hardest: Jolly Roger was dry as a bone. This is where I tried to inject some personality. I added jokes throughout the scripts, and you better believe I wore a pirate hat and eyepatch in one of the videos. (Arrr ya ready to sail the seven seas of project management?) It was a delicate balance. Too much would undermine the whole thing, so it was a light touch. We also added gag reels at the end of each video, which sparked more debate than I expected. My argument was simple: the gag reels were a small reward for anyone who watched the whole thing. Not everyone was convinced, but we moved forward.

We had over a dozen scripts. We were ready to produce them.

Chapter 5:

Producing the Series
05

Not only was I writing and performing on camera. I was also the producer of this learning path. That meant scheduling the shoot, coordinating travel for the talent (me, again), and getting stakeholder sign-off.

The first step was buy-in. Previously, my team had sent scripts to stakeholders to read and comment on asynchronously. That approach was slow, and I was still fielding a steady stream of private Jolly Roger sessions in the meantime, so I was eager to move. I borrowed a step from TV and film production: the table read. I proposed getting key stakeholders in a room to read through the scripts together, collect feedback in real time, then finish the approval process asynchronously. It worked better than expected. Beyond speeding up the approval cycle, it helped me build relationships across the org. I knew our Jolly Roger program manager was on board when she said, "I'm going to send this video to everyone who DMs me about dashboards."

With approvals in hand, I built out the shoot schedule and booked travel to our studio in northern California. Our video PM had a well-equipped space on campus: proper lighting, set dressing, the works. These videos were going to look clean. For budget reasons we broke the shoot into two trips. Our video PM was behind the camera, team members rotated in as script supervisors, and I was on camera reading from a teleprompter. My background doing on-camera commercial work came in handy here.

After each shoot, I acted as the initial screener for every episode as it came back from our video editors. The gag reels were landing. The jokes were working. The pirate hat was working. We had a solid series on our hands. Now we just needed to publish it and get it in front of people.

Chapter 6:

I'm a Star...?
06

We launched the Jolly Roger learning videos with little fanfare. We started linking them in Slack when people had questions about specific features, and sent them to anyone requesting an introduction to the tool. It was a slow burn.

After about a year, people started stopping me in the hallway on campus. "Are you the guy from the Jolly Roger videos?" Yep, that's me. They would get a little starstruck, which I found delightful every single time.

One day I was at an Apple Store getting my phone repaired when an employee walked over, wide-eyed. "You're the guy," he said.

"I am?" I asked, playing it up a little.

"From the Jolly Roger videos. I had a rotation in corporate and I watched them all. They were great."

"Oh, that's nice. Hi, I'm Ted." I reached out my hand.

"I'm Gareth. It's so cool that you get to do that for Apple!"

I was used to this happening on campus, but this was different. It was the best kind of feedback about the work I did: not a survey or an email, but a human interaction. We talked for a little while longer, then went our separate ways.

The guy from the Jolly Roger videos. I could get used to that.

Jolly Roger videos are still being watched, years after we launched. That's something I'm very proud of.
